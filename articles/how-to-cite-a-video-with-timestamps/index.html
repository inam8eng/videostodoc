How to cite a video with timestamps, and get the second right

Inam Ul Haq·Updated November 6, 2026·8 min read
The short answer

When you quote a video, you put the time right where a page number would normally go. In APA you write it like (Patel & Gupta, 2019, 1:09:19), in MLA you write (Liu 1:15), and in Chicago you put the time inside the footnote. Your full reference at the end stays the exact same as it is for the whole video. Before you finish the citation, play the video at that exact second and check your written words against the actual audio.

You are writing a paper or a story, and the best line you found came out of a video. When you quote a book, it has page numbers so your reader can go and check your quote. A one hour video has nothing like that, unless you give the reader the exact minute and second to look at.

The time goes where the page number would go. That part of the job is easy, and any citation guide will show you the format. The hard part is getting the right second, because the time mark in a transcript only shows where a caption line begins, so your actual quote might sit some way after it.

What is the timestamp format in APA, MLA and Chicago?

Academic styles need a way to point to a specific spot inside a source, and all three styles treat the time as a locator, which is just the term for the thing that tells a reader where inside a source to look. APA puts it on its own page about citing parts of a source, right alongside page numbers and tables. The MLA and Chicago examples I use below follow Scribbr's guide to citing a video.

StyleWhere the time goesExample
APAIn the in-text citation, after the year(Patel & Gupta, 2019, 1:09:19)
MLAIn the in-text citation, after the last name(Liu 1:15)
ChicagoIn the footnote, after the words educational videoeducational video, 1:01

Your reference list at the end of the paper does not change at all when you add a time mark in the text. In APA's YouTube reference format, you put the name of the account that uploaded the video in the author spot, and then you add the date, the title, the word Video in square brackets, YouTube and the link. Your teacher or editor might have their own rule on top of this, and their rule always wins.

How do I find the exact second for free?

You do not need to buy or install any tool for this job if your video is on YouTube and already has captions. Doing it by hand takes a couple of minutes for each quote, and that is completely fine when you only have a few quotes to check.

  1. Open the description box under the video and press Show transcript.
  2. Find your line in the transcript and press on it, so the video player jumps straight to that spot.
  3. Play the video and listen closely until the first word of your quote is spoken, and then read the time showing on the player.
  4. Write down that player time, and do not use the time printed beside the transcript line.
  5. Check the words in your quote against what you just heard, word by word.

This manual method stops working in two common situations. A video without captions gives you no transcript to search through, so you are stuck watching the whole thing. And if you are pulling twenty quotes across six videos, checking the audio is still quick, but hunting for the lines will take your whole evening.

Why can the time in a transcript be slightly wrong?

I learned just how far off a transcript time can be when I was building VideoDoc, because I had to decide what each time mark in the output should actually mean. In its transcript.txt file, every line starts with a time like [12:41], rounded to the whole second, and that mark shows where the caption line starts. When a caption is very short, my app joins it onto the line before it, so those later words sit after the time mark printed beside them.

The reading documents are even looser with time. In doc.md and doc.pdf, I group the lines into readable paragraphs, and a single paragraph can cover up to 60 seconds of talking with just one time mark at the top. So never cite the time beside a paragraph without checking it. Your actual quote could be spoken most of a minute after that mark.

The text in a transcript can be wrong too. YouTube's automatic captions, like speech recognition tools in general, do their worst job on names, numbers and technical terms, which are usually the exact parts you want to quote. A transcript is great for helping you find the moment, but the spoken audio is your real source.

How do I cite something shown on screen, not spoken?

Sometimes the proof you need from a video is a chart, a slide or a line of code on the screen, and the speaker never reads it out loud. A text transcript will never have that information, so you have to cite the video frame instead. MLA has a page on frame captures that was written for its eighth edition: the caption under your picture gives the source and the time, written in the form Fig. 1. Still from Berrow, See the Quiet Beauty (2:23).

Saving individual frames from the video makes this job much easier, because the image file is your evidence and it carries its own time stamp in the file name. When VideoDoc saves every frame, it names each file by its exact moment, such as frame_000123_00h01m45.50s.jpg, which sits at 1 minute and 45.5 seconds into the video. It also puts a file called frames_index.csv in the same folder to list every frame with its time, and you can set it to grab 1, 2 or 4 frames a second.

There is one legal point you should keep separate here: citing a frame tells the reader where it came from, but it does not give you permission to publish the image. For a normal class paper this is rarely an issue, but if you are publishing your work, you need to check who owns the picture first.

Quick questions

Do I have to include a timestamp when citing a video?

Yes, when you are pointing to a specific line. APA treats the time stamp as a way to point to a specific part of a source, and its page says to give that part when you quote or paraphrase something specific. If you are just mentioning the whole video in general, the normal citation without a time is enough, though your teacher or editor might still ask for more.

Should a timestamp be minutes and seconds or hours too?

You should write the numbers the exact same way the video player shows them on screen. APA's own example writes 1:09:19 for a spot past the first hour, while a moment in a shorter video is written as 1:15.

Can I cite a video transcript instead of the video?

You should cite the video itself, because that is the actual source you checked and the thing your reader can open. A caption transcript is often just a machine's rough guess at the spoken words, so use the text to find the moment, and then listen to the audio to confirm the quote.

How do I link to the exact moment in a YouTube video?

Press the Share button under the YouTube video, tick the Start at box, set your time, and copy the link it gives you. When your reader clicks that link, the player lands right on that second, but you should still keep the time written in your in-text citation so it works on printed paper too.

Go through your draft tonight, pick the one quote that your argument leans on the most, play the video at that exact second, and make sure the speaker actually says what your notes say.
