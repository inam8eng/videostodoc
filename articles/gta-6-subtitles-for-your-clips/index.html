How to add subtitles to your own GTA 6 gameplay clips for free

Inam Ul Haq·Updated November 19, 2026·8 min read
The short answer

When you record your own GTA 6 gameplay and want subtitles on the video, you have three free ways to do it. Let YouTube write automatic captions and fix the mistakes, use the caption button in a phone editor, or make an SRT file from your recording and upload it in YouTube Studio. This page is for people editing their own clips, not for the subtitle option inside the game.

Most people searching for "GTA 6 subtitles" are looking for one of two different things. Some want to turn on the subtitle setting inside the game itself, and others want Rockstar's official trailers translated into their own language. I do not cover either of those here. If you need the in-game option, check the game's settings menu or visit Rockstar Support, because I prefer not to describe menus I have not looked at myself.

Instead, I am writing for the third group of people. Rockstar set the release date for GTA 6 to 19 November 2026, so many players are recording their sessions and adding their own voice commentary. If you are doing that and want text on the screen so viewers can watch with the sound muted, you can set it up without spending money. One thing that caught me off guard early on is that "subtitles" refers to two separate formats, and choosing the wrong one can easily waste your evening.

Read this first

Before you upload anything, keep in mind that this is not legal or financial advice. GTA 6 and every piece of footage from it belong to Take-Two and Rockstar Games, and my site has no connection to them. Rockstar holds the right to take down footage whenever it chooses, so you should read its policy on posting its material before publishing your clips. Most creators make very little money from clipping at the start, and having a streamer's permission does not make those clips eligible for YouTube ads.

A subtitle file or captions in the picture: which do you need?

To pick the right format, it helps to know how the two options work. A subtitle file is a lightweight text document that sits alongside your video file, usually saved in SRT or VTT format. Each entry inside that file lists a start time, an end time, and the spoken words, allowing viewers to toggle the text on or off, which is why it is also known as closed captions. Burned-in captions work differently: the text is drawn straight onto the video frames, meaning the words stay visible permanently and nobody can turn them off.

Your choice comes down to the kind of video you are making. If you are uploading a long horizontal video to YouTube, a separate subtitle file gives you a much cleaner result. If you are cutting a short vertical clip, you will likely want the text drawn directly onto the picture, since that is standard for short-form feeds.

What are the free ways, and where do they stop?

Your first free option requires zero budget, though it does take patience. Once you upload your video, YouTube can transcribe the audio and generate captions on its own. YouTube's documentation is upfront about the accuracy, noting that automatic captions can get words wrong because of accents, dialects, and background noise, and advising creators to "Always review automatic captions" (YouTube Help). A gameplay recording is full of background noise, so plan on correcting plenty of errors.

Your second free option is to use a mobile editing app such as CapCut. You bring your clip into the app, tap the automatic captions tool, and the software draws the text right onto the video picture. Because apps frequently move features between their free and paid tiers, check inside the editor on the day you plan to use it.

Both of those approaches run into practical limits pretty quickly. YouTube only generates its captions after your upload finishes, and processing can take a while, so you cannot proofread the text before the video goes live. A phone editor works well enough for a single short clip, but transferring and editing long recordings stored on your computer becomes tedious. A subtitle file made on your own computer fills that gap.

How do you make an SRT file from your own recording?

I build a small desktop tool called VideoDoc to handle this on a computer. Its primary purpose is converting a video into a structured document with a full transcript, and starting in version 0.4.0, every completed document includes a More formats button. Both SRT and VTT are included in that menu, and you can export both for free in Lite, which is the version of the app that runs without a Pro key.

  1. Make the document. Add your recording in the Video to document tab and let it finish. The app listens to the video on your own computer. On the first run it downloads its speech engine once, about 486 MB for the Balanced one.
  2. Press More formats. The button is on the finished document, in the job list and in Your documents.
  3. Tick SRT or VTT. Press the one button to make them. The new files go into their own exports folder next to the document.
  4. Read the file once. An SRT opens in Notepad. Fix the names and the slang, because that is where a speech engine makes mistakes, and keep the time lines as they are.

The generated subtitle lines match the timing of your speech, so each line appears on screen right as you say it. If you load a clip that contains zero talking, the app lets you know there is no speech and skips creating an empty file. Knowing this saves time with gameplay footage, because a subtitle file only captures spoken words and will ignore game sound effects or background music.

How do you upload the file in YouTube Studio?

Once you have your file, YouTube accepts both formats. According to its list of supported caption files, YouTube accepts SubRip (.srt) and WebVTT (.vtt), though it only reads the plain, basic versions and ignores styling. Because of that, you do not need to waste effort adjusting fonts or colors inside the text file.

  1. Open YouTube Studio and select Subtitles from the menu on the left.
  2. Select your video and set the language that you speak in the recording.
  3. Inside the subtitles section, click Add and then select Upload file.
  4. Choose With timing, since your file already includes the timestamps, and select your SRT file.
  5. Play through a few lines to check that the text matches your voice, and then hit publish.

YouTube updates its Studio layout from time to time, so the exact menu names can shift slightly, and you can check the latest instructions on the Add subtitles and captions help page. If you notice the words showing up too early or too late, you fix that by adjusting the timestamps inside the text file rather than re-editing your video.

What about captions burned into a short?

For a vertical clip, the second tab in the same app does that job. Video to shorts cuts a long video into shorts and puts word by word captions on them by default, with the spoken word highlighted, in three styles called Bold, Clean and Big. Lite takes videos up to 20 minutes and adds a small "Made with VideoDoc" mark to each export, and Pro has no length limit and no mark. I wrote the full walk through on a separate page, GTA 6 shorts from one long video.

Keep in mind that adding captions will not change whether your clip can earn money. Text on the screen helps viewers follow what you are saying, and I will not promise anything beyond that. If you are cutting clips from someone else's stream, YouTube's channel monetization policies state that the reused content rule still applies even if the original streamer told you it was fine. Adding your own voice commentary or telling your own story is what makes a clip original, and subtitles by themselves do not count.

Honest limits

I also want to be clear about what the software cannot do. VideoDoc transcribes the spoken audio in the same language it was recorded, so I am not claiming it translates your subtitles into foreign languages. The app has no built-in knowledge of the game, so the names of people and places will often be spelled wrong until you correct them by hand. Processing long recordings in the document tab requires Pro, so try out Lite on a short personal clip first, and note that the app only runs on Windows and Apple Silicon Macs.

Quick questions

Is this about the subtitle setting inside GTA 6?

No, this page covers adding subtitles to gameplay videos that you recorded yourself. If you want to turn on subtitles inside the game, open the game's settings menu or check Rockstar Support.

Is SRT or VTT better for YouTube?

Both formats work well on YouTube. YouTube's documentation lists both as supported and reads only the plain text and timing from each, ignoring any custom styling, though SRT is supported by more external video tools.

Do subtitles make my clips eligible for ads?

No, captions make your video easier to follow, but they do not change YouTube's monetization rules. You still need your own commentary or story to make the clip original, and Rockstar's content policy continues to apply to the game footage.

Grab one short clip that has your own voice on it, generate the SRT file tonight, and read through the text once before you upload it.
