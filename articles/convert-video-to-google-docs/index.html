How to convert a video to a Google Doc and keep the pictures

Inam Ul Haq·Updated November 10, 2026·8 min read
The short answer

Google Docs cannot open a video file directly, so getting a video into a Doc means you have to extract the spoken words as text and add the screen pictures yourself. If you only care about the speech, you can just paste a transcript. If you need both the words and the screen images, you create a file that holds both, convert that into a DOCX using Pandoc, and open that DOCX in Google Docs.

You probably have a recorded lecture or an internal training video, and your team wants it inside a Google Doc so people can read along and add comments. You go looking for a tool online, and most of what you find is just a website that transcribes the audio into plain text. When your video is simply a speaker talking to the camera, that text transcript is enough.

When the video shows someone sharing their screen, the raw transcript ends up full of phrases like "click here" or "as you can see", and you cannot see what the speaker was pointing at. People often try to make a PDF with the pictures and open that in Google Docs, but Google's own support notes mention that this conversion works best on files of 2 MB or smaller. If your video shows a screen, make a DOCX first and open that in Google Docs.

What is the free way to get a video's words into Google Docs?

When your video is on YouTube and already has closed captions, YouTube has transcribed the audio for you, so you do not need to convert anything. You just need to copy those lines over to your document.

  1. Open the video on YouTube, go to the description box below the player, and click on Show transcript.
  2. Highlight the text lines in that side panel and copy them.
  3. Open a blank Google Doc and paste the lines there.
  4. Delete any timestamps you do not need, and type in a clear section title whenever the topic moves on.

This method is free, but it runs into two clear walls. If a video does not have captions, you get no text at all, and it will never capture the images from the screen. If you have an audio or video file sitting on your hard drive, my free browser version transcribes it locally on your computer and lets you save a .txt or .md file. It downloads a small model of about 80 MB on the first run, once, and it still gives you only the spoken words.

Can Google Docs voice typing transcribe a video?

A common suggestion online is to turn on Google Docs voice typing and let it listen to your video playing through your speakers. Google's voice typing page notes that your computer microphone must be active, so your microphone is merely picking up whatever sound comes out of the speakers. A one hour recording takes a full hour to finish, and every background noise in the room gets picked up too. I would only rely on it for a few minutes of speech when I have no other choice.

What does Google Docs do to a PDF full of pictures?

Inside Google Drive, you can right-click any PDF and select Open with Google Docs, which seems like an easy path. That feature relies on OCR, which stands for optical character recognition, meaning Google scans the image of each page to detect the letters. It was designed to pull text from scanned paper, and Google's page on converting PDFs outlines the real boundaries of what it can do.

  • The PDF file should be 2 MB or smaller.
  • The letters must be at least 10 pixels in height.
  • Font styling like bold, italics, font size, typeface, and line breaks are likely to be kept.
  • Structured items like tables, lists, multi-column layouts, footnotes, and endnotes are not likely to be detected.

Google's documentation does not explain what happens to embedded images during this process, and I will not pretend to know. I do know that a document carrying a few dozen screen grabs can cross that 2 MB line very fast. You can certainly test it on a tiny file and inspect the first few pages, but I would not build a working routine around it.

How do I get the words and the screen pictures into a Google Doc?

Because Google Docs cannot process raw video, you have to split the job into two clear steps. First, you assemble a source file that contains both the text and the matching screen images, and then you convert that into a DOCX, which is the standard Word format that Google Docs can open.

I want to be completely direct about what my own software does. VideoDoc does not generate Google Docs directly, and there is no direct export button for Google. I originally wrote it so an AI tool could read through the lectures I study, which is why it outputs PDF and Markdown files. You give it a link to a public video or select a local file, it processes everything locally on your machine, and it produces doc.pdf, doc.md, and a folder of images. The spoken words carry timestamps, and each screenshot is placed right where it appeared in the clip.

Markdown is simply structured plain text that uses basic symbols for headings and picture links. Google Docs can import a Markdown file, but the generated doc.md file only contains links to the images in that separate folder, so do not count on your pictures appearing in the Doc that way. Pandoc is a free command-line conversion utility, and its manual shows docx as a supported target format. It packages the written text and the image files together into one complete file.

  1. Download and install Pandoc from its official site.
  2. Open the folder containing your doc.md file and the images folder, and make sure they stay in that same place together.
  3. If you are on Windows, click inside the folder address bar, type cmd, and hit Enter. If you use a Mac, open your Terminal in that folder.
  4. Type pandoc doc.md -o doc.docx and press Enter.
  5. Upload the resulting doc.docx file into your Google Drive.
  6. Right-click that uploaded file, select Open with Google Docs, and check through the document to make sure your pictures came through.

When your images do not show up, it almost always means you ran the Pandoc command from the wrong folder, so open the prompt inside the exact folder where doc.md lives and run it once more. File size is the other thing you need to watch. Google's file limits allow up to 1.02 million characters in a document and a limit of 50 MB for converted text documents, and transcript text from a long video will never come close to that. The screen images are what inflate the file size, so if your file exceeds 50 MB, keep doc.pdf stored in Drive and link back to it from a lightweight Doc containing the text.

Quick questions

Can you upload a video straight into Google Docs?

No, you cannot. Google Docs is built for text documents and cannot parse video files, which means the audio has to be turned into text beforehand. You can still paste a link to the video inside your Doc so your team can open and watch it.

Does opening a PDF in Google Docs keep the pictures?

Google's documentation makes no guarantees about images. It says your PDF should be 2 MB or smaller and notes that tables and lists are not likely to be detected. When your file contains a lot of screen captures, converting through DOCX is the more reliable choice.

How big can a Google Doc be?

Google sets a limit of 1.02 million characters for a Doc, and up to 50 MB for converted text files. The spoken text from even a very long recording will fit without issues. It is the screenshots and pictures that push you toward that size cap.

Does VideoDoc export to Google Docs?

No, it does not. The software generates a PDF, a Markdown file, and a folder of screenshots locally on your computer. You bring those files into Google Docs yourself by following the conversion steps outlined above.

Take one video that involves screen sharing, run the Pandoc conversion on it this evening, and check the first three pages in Google Docs before relying on the rest of the file.
