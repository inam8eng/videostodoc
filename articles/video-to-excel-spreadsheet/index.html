How to turn a video into an Excel spreadsheet you can sort and filter

Inam Ul Haq·Updated November 26, 2026·8 min read
The short answer

Excel cannot open a video file directly, but you can turn the contents of that video into spreadsheet rows. The setup that actually helps puts one shot on each row, along with its start time, the spoken words and a small picture of the screen. If the video is on YouTube and has captions, you can copy and paste the transcript for free. VideoDoc can also export this for you as a free CSV file, or as a complete Excel workbook in Pro.

Suppose you are sitting with a long recording, maybe a training session or a two hour meeting, and you want to work with it in Excel. You might want to find every spot where a specific word came up, mark off sections as you review them, or share a list of key moments with a colleague. When you search online for how to turn a video into Excel, most of the results solve a completely different problem.

Those search results usually show how to embed a video player inside a sheet, or how to scan a data table that happens to appear on screen. You will also run into hobby scripts that turn video frames into coloured spreadsheet cells. None of those approaches give you rows you can filter and sort. What you actually need is one row for every part of the video, with its time and its words.

I build a small desktop tool called VideoDoc that converts videos into readable documents, and starting with version 0.4.0, I added spreadsheet exports as well. Here I will walk through the free manual method first, then show you what my Excel file actually contains, and finally be upfront about what it cannot do.

What does a video look like once it is in Excel?

There are two pieces of information in a video that make sense in a spreadsheet: what people said, and what was showing on the screen. The spoken part is the transcript, which is just the speech written out line by line with a timestamp next to each line. The visual part is the shots, meaning each stretch of the video where the picture stays mostly unchanged, such as a single presentation slide or one window of a program.

Once those two parts are sitting in rows, you can use Excel for the things it handles best. You can filter the spoken words column for "invoice" and immediately see every moment that word was mentioned, along with its exact time. You can also sort the shots by duration so the slides that stayed on screen the longest move straight to the top. On top of that, you have room to add your own columns right next to the data for notes, an owner, or a simple done check.

The free way, and where it stops

If the video is already up on YouTube and has captions turned on, you do not need to install anything. YouTube keeps the spoken words paired with their timestamps, and you can grab them directly.

  1. Open the video page on YouTube and expand the description box below the video player.
  2. Click Show transcript, and a side panel will appear listing every spoken line next to its timestamp.
  3. Highlight all the text inside that panel, copy it, and paste it into cell A1 of a blank worksheet.
  4. Insert a header row at the top, and then switch on the filter from the Data tab.

This method is completely free, but you run into its limits pretty quickly. The timestamps and the spoken lines usually paste into the same column on alternating rows, so you have to separate them manually before sorting works properly. You also get zero pictures of the screen. And if the video has no captions, or if it is a local file sitting on your own computer, this option is not available at all.

There is another free route that saves you from cleaning up pasted text. VideoDoc Lite, which is the free version of my app, exports the same breakdown as CSV files. A CSV is just a plain text file where each line acts as a row and commas separate the columns, so Excel opens it straight up like a normal sheet. You generate a document from your video first, click More formats on that document, and tick CSV.

What is in the CSV files?

VideoDoc places two files inside a folder named exports right next to your document. The first file, transcript.csv, puts one spoken line on each row, while shots.csv puts one shot on each row. Both files include the time in two columns: one in plain seconds so you can sort cleanly, and another formatted like 0:01:45 so it is easy to read.

I always include the hours digit in that readable time, because Excel has a frustrating habit here. If you give Excel 00:06, it treats that as six minutes rather than six seconds, so short timestamps break the second you open the file. Writing it out as 0:00:06 makes Excel read it properly.

The second problem I ran into during development was other scripts. When you open a standard CSV containing Urdu, Hindi, Arabic or Chinese in Excel, the text often turns into question marks because Excel guesses the wrong alphabet. To stop that from happening, VideoDoc writes a tiny hidden marker at the very beginning of the file, called a byte order mark, which tells Excel the file uses UTF-8 encoding, so those languages open properly with no extra import steps.

The one thing a CSV file cannot do is store images inside the cells. The picture column only lists the file name of the screenshot from that moment, so if you want to see the screen, you still have to open that image file yourself.

What does the Excel workbook add?

The full Excel export comes with Pro, and it gives you a single workbook named document.xlsx containing three sheets. The main difference compared to the CSV is that the screenshots sit right inside the workbook, and the layout is already prepared before you open it.

On the Shots sheet, the Picture column displays a small copy of the screen directly inside the row, and the Narration column holds whatever was spoken during that shot. The top header row is frozen so it stays visible while you scroll, and the filter arrows are switched on by default. If the video is in a right to left language like Urdu or Arabic, the sheets are set to open right to left as well.

Two small fixes came directly out of testing before version 0.4.0 went out. Testers tried to break the new spreadsheet exports, and they noticed that if a spoken line happened to start with an equals sign, Excel could try to run it as a formula. Because of that, VideoDoc now stores all text from a video strictly as plain text so it never executes as a formula. The other limit is that a single Excel cell can only hold 32,767 characters, which is listed on Microsoft's Excel limits page, so if a long stretch of speech goes past that, it continues into the next row instead of getting cut off.

And if what you really want is a list of every saved frame along with its exact timestamp, that uses a separate file, which I walk through in how to read a video frames index.

Honest limits

There are a few clear limits to keep in mind. This will not convert a table shown inside a video into live spreadsheet cells, so if someone shares a spreadsheet or a price list on screen, you get a screenshot of it in the row rather than editable numbers. The Excel workbook embeds small pictures for the first 400 shots, and any shots after that still keep their timestamps and spoken words without an image. Video links only work for public videos, whereas a file saved on your own computer always works. Finally, the text is only as accurate as the captions or the speech engine that picked it up, so double check important names and numbers before you trust a filter.

Quick questions

Can Excel open a video file?

No. Excel can hold a link to a video or show one inside a workbook, but it cannot read what is said or shown in it. To work with a video in Excel you first turn it into rows, which means a transcript with times, a list of shots, or both.

Is the CSV really free?

Yes. In VideoDoc Lite the free formats are SRT, VTT, CSV and JSON. The Excel workbook, Word and PowerPoint are part of Pro.

Can it read a table from the screen into cells?

No. A table shown in the video stays a picture. You get that picture next to the words spoken while it was on screen, and typing the numbers into cells is still up to you.
